The Lineup

Keith Creelman built a lineup with one genuinely elite piece in it and then filled the rest in by hand. Puka Nacua and Christian Watson give him a receiver room ranked 4 in the league, and that ranking is almost entirely Nacua — Watson is a body the market does not rate holding down the other side. The combined figure of 12,549 is the best thing on this roster and the least balanced.

The backfield is the sturdier half. Saquon Barkley and Travis Etienne sit 6 among the running back groups, with TreVeyon Henderson behind them in a flex, which means three backs Creelman is happy to start and no obvious hole between them. The running back value of 10,861 is spread across bodies rather than propped up by one. Parker Washington takes the other flex.

Then it gets thin. Dak Prescott and Kyler Murray in the superflex put the quarterback pairing at 8, which is a strange place to land for a man who spent the way he spent. Dallas Goedert is the tight end room, all of it, and 851 is what that produces — a rank of 9 at the position.

Overall the composite lands at 6 of 12, on a score of 53,538. Nacua and Barkley carry it; Goedert and Christian Watson drag on it. Cam Little kicks, Tommy Townsend punts, and Josh Hines-Allen, Jalyx Hunt and Nick Bolton are all startable on defence without anyone pretending they are the reason this team wins.

The bench is where the odd money sits. Marvin Harrison Jr is the best thing not starting, worth roughly what Parker Washington is worth in the flex ahead of him, and Stefon Diggs is on there at the league minimum, which is the best-priced thing Creelman did all offseason. Jacoby Brissett and Chig Okonkwo are also on there, at prices that are not the minimum, which is the second half of that sentence and less flattering.

The Bridge

Creelman opened the offseason with Deshaun Watson at quarterback and Emanuel Wilson at running back, which tells you everything about the starting point. Tetairoa McMillan was the only starter the market rated as a top-tier player at his position, TreVeyon Henderson led the backfield, and Trey Benson and Keon Coleman were occupying flex and superflex slots the board barely recognises. The offense graded out at 9,268, with 5 of the nine priced slots unfilled in any meaningful sense.

Contracts expire in this league and rosters empty out; a spring lineup that reads like a waiver page is the middle of the cycle, not a fire. Creelman kept his franchise, kept Goedert, Henderson and Christian Watson, and went shopping for the other six slots. The active-roster salary at that point was $62,000.

By the time the auction opened, the shape of the year had already changed once. McMillan was gone and Puka Nacua was in the WR1 slot — the same stud count, an entirely different player, and the largest single upgrade Creelman made all offseason hidden inside a number that did not move. Travis Etienne had arrived and pushed Henderson to RB2, KC Concepcion had taken a flex and Kayshon Boutte the superflex. The offense read 19,237 against an active salary of $95,000.

The auction did the rest, loudly. Dak Prescott replaced Deshaun Watson, Saquon Barkley took RB1 and pushed Etienne and Henderson down a rung each, and Kyler Murray filled the superflex that Boutte had been standing in. Parker Washington now holds the second flex. The lineup value finished at 33,481, the studs went to 2, and the holes came down to 2.

Those remaining two are Christian Watson and Parker Washington, and they are the difference between this team and the ones above it. This is not a roster that is adequate everywhere — it is two elite starters, a solid middle, and two slots where the market has no opinion worth having. Committed cap now sits at $297,000 across 30 active players, with $3,000 of room left to fix anything.

Keep the two questions apart. Very little of what Creelman starts today was carried on an existing contract — Goedert, Christian Watson and Henderson are the survivors, and none of them is why this lineup ranks where it does. Nacua came by trade, Prescott and Barkley and Murray came at auction. Whatever this team is, he made it this year.

The Auction

Creelman bought four quarterbacks. Dak Prescott went for $63,000, which is the single largest bill of his auction by a distance and accounts for 31.8% of everything he spent. Kyler Murray followed at real money and starts in the superflex, so that pair is defensible. Jacoby Brissett and Mac Jones are the ones that need explaining, because both are on this roster and neither is in the lineup.

Across 24 winning lots he laid out $198,000, and Saquon Barkley was the other genuinely big ticket — a stud in the RB1 slot from a standing start, which is exactly what the money is for. Beneath that, the shopping is sensible and cheap: Nick Bolton and Josh Hines-Allen are both startable defenders bought at prices that will not hurt anyone, and Tommy Townsend punts. Will McDonald was bought at auction and then dropped as the make-room leg of a waiver claim before the season, which is the kind of round trip that makes a bid look ornamental.

The buy that grates is Chig Okonkwo. Creelman paid at auction for a tight end, then handed him a multi-year deal at a salary well above the league minimum, and Dallas Goedert still starts. Okonkwo is bench value on a bench contract, and the tight end rank did not move an inch for it.

Contracts, Trades, Cuts and the Rookie Class

Creelman cut Trey Benson off a contract that carried a penalty and then re-signed him at the league minimum weeks later, which is either patient or lucky and works out the same on the ledger. He made 12 contract moves in all, every one of them a Multi-Year Contract except Puka Nacua's, which was a Restructure — the existing money re-based over the years already on the deal, not new seasons bolted on the back. Cam Little, Brian Robinson, Charlie Kolar, Jack Strand, Jacob Saylors, Stefon Diggs and Benson all landed at the league minimum. Parker Washington and Chig Okonkwo did not, and neither did Kayshon Boutte or Michael Mayer, all four of whom sit above it.

Diggs at the minimum is the best-priced thing in the whole set — bench value at the floor of the salary scale. Washington's deal is the one with a starting slot attached to it, and the market rates that slot a hole.

The trades number 6 and split cleanly. Kenneth Walker went to HammerTime for their natural first-rounder in next year's draft — talent out, future asset in, no cap logic to it. The Gride deal was the efficient one: a first-round pick out, and Travis Etienne plus the pick that became KC Concepcion back, straightforwardly talent-driven and straightforwardly good. Two more with Pure Greatness and Gride were pure pick shuffling, moving up in the second and third at the cost of a future third and a pair of later selections.

The three-way with C-Town Chivalry and Blake Bombers is the one that defines the roster. Creelman sent out Tetairoa McMillan, his own natural first-rounder next year, Chris Rodriguez and Omar Cooper, and got Puka Nacua — four assets condensed into one, which is a talent-driven trade in the plainest sense. It was not a two-man swap: C-Town also sent Drake London and blind-bid money to Blake Bombers, which is the part that makes the price look like a price. Having acquired HammerTime's first-rounder in the spring, he then spent his own on Nacua, so he still has one. The last deal, George Karlaftis and a future fifth to the Hawks for a future fourth, moved a startable defender for a small upgrade in draft slot and barely registers.

Removals came to 20 bodies, of which only 5 were contracts carried into the league year and released. Noah Fant was depth behind Goedert. Blake Watson was a non-contributor and cost nothing to remove. Benson and Keon Coleman were technically holding priced slots, which flatters them — both were rated outside anything the board cares about, and replacing them was the whole point. The penalties charged against this season total $3,750; another $2,000 lands next year and has no bearing on the cap he is working with now. Add 12 drops that were just the make-room leg of a claim and 3 bodies signed and discarded inside the same offseason, and the churn is mostly noise.

The rookie class runs to 5 picks. KC Concepcion came from a slot that has historically produced a usable player more often than not, and he is the only one of the group with real redraft value attached. Omar Cooper Jr. came from a slot that is close to a coin flip and was shipped out in the Nacua deal before he played a down for Creelman. De'Zhaun Stribling was the move-up in the second, from a slot that hits far less often, and he currently prices out near Concepcion. Sonny Styles and Zion Young are off the board entirely — for a third-rounder that is roughly what the slot promises, and for a sixth there is nothing to measure against at all. None of them have played a game; this is what the slots have historically been worth, not a grade.

The Verdict

Creelman replaced every priced slot he owns except Christian Watson and Dallas Goedert, and finished 6 overall. So: no, this is not the strongest team in the league, and nothing about the middle of this roster argues otherwise. But the offseason was a good one, and those two facts sit comfortably together — he started from an April lineup with Emanuel Wilson in it and he is now a plausible playoff team.

The biggest advantage is Puka Nacua, and it is not close. He is the reason the receiver group grades 4, he cost four assets and a future first, and he is the one player on this roster who wins a week on his own. Saquon Barkley behind him makes the running back room 6, which is the only other place this team is comfortably above the middle.

The weakness is Dallas Goedert, alone at tight end, and the position rank of 9 that comes with him. Behind that sits the quarterback problem: Prescott and Murray at 8 in a superflex league, after Creelman bought four quarterbacks and paid a fortune for one of them. He spent the most money in his auction on the position where he still ranks worst outside tight end. That is the bill for the year in one line.
