The Board

Bear Dunn asked for this. In the middle of August, in the chat where these twelve argue all year, he wanted to know whether the bot could hand the league a preseason forecast. It can, and it likes him better than anybody else in the room: C-Town Chivalry first, 18.1% to win the whole thing.

Can the bot give us preseason predictions for the league? Forecast?— Bear Dunn, C-Town Chivalry · August 15, 2026, #the-coffee-shop

The rest of the board is less obliging. Brian Cutting's Pure Greatness, the defending champion, is second. Shawn Blake is third, a hair ahead of Eric Martel, who gets Josh Jacobs back from his suspension in week three. Then it flattens out: Keith Creelman, Josh Martel, Derrick Whitman and Eric Mannila are stacked between 50.8% and 48.4%, close enough that one good October reshuffles all four. Brian Cross, Chris Klingenberg and Ryan Bousquet trail, and Gerardi's Gride sits by itself at the bottom, 20.4% to make the playoffs.

I was promised a season preview. I am prepared to be torn apart.— Shawn Blake, Blake Bombers · September 9, 2026, #the-coffee-shop

Don't be fooled by how close the averages look. Average a team's big years with its bad ones and everybody drifts toward the middle, but real seasons never finish that bunched up. In the 16 seasons this league has played, the all-play leader has averaged 73.3% of his all-play games and the last-place team 22.9%.

C-Town is first in the league on offense and first on defense, and that is the whole case for Dunn. Below him it gets strange. Blake bought Josh Allen at the auction and starts the best quarterback in the league. Eric Martel traded Jalen Hurts away in April, never replaced him, and starts the worst -- throwing to the best receivers and the best tight end in the league. Cross has the best defensive line and the best linebackers and still sits 9th, because the offense around Hurts is thin: his receivers and his flexes both rank eleventh. And Gride is in the league's top half at exactly one spot: receiver.

Mannila is the one that looks wrong. He starts the third-most valuable offense in the league, and the forecast has him 8th. Value is what his players would fetch on the redraft market; the forecast runs on MFL's weekly projections instead, and MFL is cooler than the market on CeeDee Lamb, Malik Nabers and Tucker Kraft. In the weekly points the forecast runs on, the table below, his offense is seventh, his defense is level with CBP's for the league's worst and his kicker and punter together rank tenth; the bottom four defenses there are separated by a fraction of a point. Nine of the eighteen slots a team starts are defenders, a kicker and a punter. Nobody wins a league there -- the gap from the best defense to the worst is a fraction of the gap on offense -- but a team can give away a point or two a week at nine positions at once, and those are the easiest points in the league to get back.

April: What Twelve Teams Had

Eric Martel made the one spring trade that changed a contender. In April he sent Jalen Hurts and blind-bid money to Cross for The Long Haulers' 2027 first and second. Then he spent his own first- and second-round picks for next year on two running backs, Kenneth Walker from Creelman and David Montgomery from L.A. Looks, and restructured Walker and Ja'Marr Chase to make room. The Hurts trade is why his quarterbacks now rank last in the league.

April is where you see who was getting his money's worth. A table in the auction section below sets each owner's slice of the league's payroll against his slice of the league's starting-offense value. Payroll includes defenders and the value count does not, so a team that spent on defense looks a little worse there than it really is. Blake held a small slice of the salary and a much bigger slice of the value. Cutting and Dunn paid above-average payrolls and still held more value than they paid for. Josh Martel and Cross carried the two biggest payrolls and were paying for more than they were starting. Creelman had almost no salary and almost no team -- which turned out to be a plan, not a problem: nobody spent more at the auction.

Most of the tags did not stick. The Two Auctions

Josh Allen went to Blake for $88,000, the priciest player of the summer, after fifteen bids, and the last man Blake had to outbid was Creelman. Blake's was not the biggest bill, though. Creelman spent more than any owner in the league and walked out with Dak Prescott, Saquon Barkley and Kyler Murray. Whitman spent nearly as much and bought an entire backfield, Tony Pollard included -- the same Tony Pollard that C-Town had cut on lock day to make room.

Across the Free Agent Auction and the smaller Expired Rookie Auction the twelve bought 196 players for $1,352,000, and almost all of it, $1,315,000, went at the Free Agent Auction. Even the punters had a bidding war: Blake pushed up the price on Ryan Eckley before Josh Martel took him. Thirteen days did nearly the whole year's work. Whatever a roster looked like at the lock in late July, it looked like a football team by the close in early August.

Defense was where the money was not. Only Cross and Eric Martel put a serious share of their budget into defenders, and it worked: The Long Haulers start the best defensive line and the best linebackers in the league. Everybody else bought offense. Everybody else was probably right.

Appendix: How the Forecast Is Calculated

This is how the forecast works, and how it would have done. The projections are MFL's own: projected points for every player in every week, under this league's scoring.

Each simulated week the model starts every team's best lineup from those projections. Players MFL lists as out sit for the weeks they are expected to miss, and so do two whose injuries had been reported but not yet listed. At every position a waiver-level player is available to every team, and he starts wherever he projects better than what the team has, because an owner with a hole or a weak spot picks somebody up. The model keeps only 70.0% of each team's projected edge over the league, because preseason favorites fall back toward the pack more often than they pull away. Then it adds luck twice: once for the whole season, since some teams simply are better or worse than their projections, and once every week. It plays the real schedule, crowns the division winners, runs the six-team bracket, and does all of that 20,000 times.

The fair test of a forecast is whether it would have worked before. A simpler version of the same model was replayed on 2021 through 2025. It used each player's week-one projection for the whole season, with no injury hold-outs, because MFL's archived projections for later weeks were made during the season and already knew each week's injury news, which a preseason forecast could not have. Across those 60 team-seasons its projections accounted for 40.1% of the variation in final all-play: it saw a real part of what was coming and missed more than half of it. Its typical miss was 11.8 points of all-play, against 15.3 if you simply called every team a coin flip.

The order is the weak part. It had 2021 and 2022 nearly right, scoring 0.90 and 0.87 on a scale where one is the exact order and zero is no relation at all. The last three came in at 0.42, 0.31 and 0.34: only loosely right. Last season it had Sex Manther dead last, and Josh Martel finished runner-up.

Its settings were tuned on those same seasons, so read what follows as a check, not proof. Kept whole, the projected gaps between teams came out too big: the real gaps were only about two thirds of them. Trimmed to 70.0%, the projected gaps matched the real ones almost exactly and the typical miss was at its smallest. The playoff odds held up: teams it gave long odds rarely made it, and teams it gave strong odds usually did. And 48 of the 60 teams finished inside their own big-year to bad-year range, though the luck in the model was sized to those seasons, so the ranges were always likely to fit.

This year is harder to call than any season it was tested on. The typical team projects 7.2 all-play points from the league average on week-one projections, against at least 9.4 in every tested season, and 5.9 on the projections this forecast uses, against at least 7.4. To spread the finish out like a real season, the model has to add more luck: a team's whole season can run about 14.0 fantasy points a week above or below its projection, more than any tested year needed (the most was 12.9). More of this forecast is luck and less of it is rosters.

What it cannot do. It sees nothing that happens after it runs: no trades, no pickups beyond its waiver stand-ins, no new injuries. It starts the best lineup every week, which owners do not. It uses one projection source, and MFL disagrees with the market on some stars. And it grades rosters, not the men who run them.
