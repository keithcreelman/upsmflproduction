Bear Dunn Asked for a Forecast. It Came Back With His Name on Top.

Bear Dunn asked the league for a forecast in August. It came back with his team first, the defending champion second and Matt Gerardi's Gride alone at the bottom -- and with a middle five teams deep that it cannot separate. The summer cost $1,352,000 and left $130,000 in the whole league to change any of it.

The Board

Bear Dunn asked for this. In the middle of August, in the chat where these twelve argue all year, he wanted to know whether the bot could hand the league a preseason forecast. It can. It has. And it likes him better than it likes anybody else in the room: C-Town Chivalry first, 20.8% to win the whole thing.

The rest of the board is less obliging. Brian Cutting's Pure Greatness, the defending champion, is second. Shawn Blake is third, with the most expensive player of the summer on his roster. Then it flattens out. Eric Martel, Keith Creelman, Josh Martel, Derrick Whitman and Eric Mannila are stacked on top of one another between 52.2% and 49.1%, which is a technical way of saying a good October reorders them. Brian Cross, Chris Klingenberg and Ryan Bousquet trail. Gerardi's Gride sits by itself at the bottom, 20.9% to make the playoffs.

Read the board across, not down. The all-play column is an average of 20,000 seasons, so it bunches; the two columns beside it are the same team in a big year and in a bad one, and they are miles apart. Dunn averages 59.8% and his good year is better than anything on the page. That is what a real season looks like: in the 16 this league has played, the team that led all-play won 73.3% of its matchups and the team that finished last won 22.9%.

Below the top line the talent is scattered strangely. C-Town is first in the league on offense and first on defense, which is the entire case for the top seed. Blake paid $88,000 for Josh Allen, the most anyone paid for anyone this summer, and starts the best quarterbacks in the league. Eric Martel traded Jalen Hurts away in April, never replaced him, and starts the worst -- behind the best receivers and the best tight end in the league. Cross owns the best defensive line and the best linebackers and sits 9th, because his receivers are thin. And there is exactly one column on either board where Gride is in the league's top half: the receivers.

Which brings up the board's sharpest argument. Mannila starts the third-most valuable offense in the league and the forecast has him 8th, which looks wrong until you see where the points come from. Value is a market price; the forecast runs on MFL's weekly projections, and those rank his offense sixth, his defense tenth and his kicker and punter eleventh. Nine of the eighteen slots a team starts are defenders, a kicker and a punter. Nobody wins a league there -- the gap from the best defense to the worst is a fraction of the gap between the best offense and the worst -- but a team can quietly give away a few points a week at nine positions at once, and that is Mannila's season.

April: What Twelve Teams Had

Eric Martel spent April taking the best thing he had and turning it into two other things. He sent Jalen Hurts and blind-bid money to Cross for The Long Haulers' 2027 first and second, then spent two 2027 picks of his own on Kenneth Walker, bought from Creelman, and on David Montgomery, and restructured Ja'Marr Chase and Walker to pay for it. It is the one spring move that changed a contender. It is also why his quarterback room now ranks last in the league.

What April is good for is seeing who was getting value for money, and the table below puts every owner's payroll beside his talent as a share of the league's. Blake held a small slice of the salary and a much bigger slice of the value; so did Cutting and Dunn. Josh Martel and Cross were paying for more than they were starting. Creelman had almost no salary committed and almost no team -- which, as it turned out, was a position rather than a problem.

The Draft and the Tags

Eric Martel opened the league's first Owner Divisional Draft by taking Cross, and under the rules of the thing that was not a compliment. The owners had voted in May to pick their own divisions: four captains, two rounds, a snake. They ran it first that night, before a single rookie was drafted. The bot spelled out the logic the next morning. Getting picked first means your captain thinks you are the weakest team on the board.

Bousquet welcomed Cross to the honour by telling him it was going to be a long haul. Cross, whose team is called The Long Haulers, said he had at least chosen the right name when he joined.

Only then did they draft players. 72 rookie picks moved that night, and the trading was busier than the drafting. Eric Martel moved up twice. Creelman traded three times and spent one of the picks he got on Sonny Styles less than a minute after acquiring it -- a pick that had belonged to CBP, then L.A. Looks, then CBP again, then Sex Manther, then The Long Haulers, then Gride for about an hour.

The order of it is in the table. Two things in it are worth saying out loud. Dunn took the Hawks 3rd, exactly as he had said he would in January, four months before there was a draft to say it about. And the last team on the board was the defending champion: Pure Greatness sat there until 8th, when Eric Martel took it, which is how one division ended up holding the last two champions.

The tags were chips rather than keeps. Amon-Ra St. Brown was tagged and traded. George Pickens was tagged in May and released on lock day, the money going straight back to Dunn. Christian McCaffrey was tagged by Josh Martel, handed back in July and bought at the auction by Dunn for barely more than the tag had cost. The ones that stuck were the quiet ones: Kamren Curl, Myles Garrett, Javonte Williams, Trevor Lawrence.

The Two Auctions

Blake paid $88,000 for Josh Allen after fifteen bids, beating the commissioner to him, and that was the biggest single check anyone wrote this summer. It was not the biggest bill. Creelman spent more than any owner in the league and walked out with Dak Prescott, Saquon Barkley and Kyler Murray. Whitman spent nearly as much and bought an entire backfield, Tony Pollard included -- the same Tony Pollard that C-Town had cut on lock day to make room.

Across the two auctions the twelve bought 196 players for $1,352,000. Almost all of it, $1,315,000, was the Free Agent Auction. Thirteen days did nearly the whole year's work: whatever a roster looked like at the lock in late July, it looked like a football team by the close in early August.

The money did not go where the talent already was. Two of the three biggest gains went to the owners who arrived with the least -- Whitman, who had the fewest players in the league, and Creelman, who had the smallest salary committed in it. Dunn gained the most of anyone and he had walked in with the second-best roster, but Cutting and Mannila, holding two of the other best lineups, spent about as little as anybody and finished the summer where they started.

The share table tells the rest, and it is the most useful thing on this page. Payroll is now almost perfectly level: every owner in the league carries within a point or so of a twelfth of the league's salary. Talent is not. Dunn holds a far bigger slice of the league's lineup value than of its payroll, Mannila too on the strength of spending almost nothing at auction, while the Hawks, L.A. Looks and Gride hold a twelfth of the salary and about half a twelfth of the value. The league bought itself level on money and stayed unequal on players.

Defense was where the money was not. Only Cross and Eric Martel put a serious share of their budget into defenders, and it worked: The Long Haulers start the best defensive line and the best linebackers in the league. Everybody else bought offense. Everybody else was probably right.

Since the Auction

Creelman made fourteen waiver pickups before week one, more than any owner in the league, most of them one-for-one swaps at the very back of his roster. One was Trey Benson, whom he had cut in July and claimed back less than three weeks later. Josh Martel had a view on all that activity.

There is more room there than anyone is using, and it is not on offense. The wire holds none of the league's best at any offensive position -- offense is bought in July or traded for, and that is the end of it. But 6 of the twelve best projected defensive backs are sitting on the wire right now, along with 5 of the twelve best kickers and 4 of the twelve best punters, including the best-projected punter in the league. The cheapest points available to anybody this season are defensive, and they cost a waiver claim.

Otherwise nothing much has moved. The league has made 42 waiver and free-agent pickups since the auction closed and put through three trades, and the twelve starting lineups came out of five weeks about where the auction left them. There is no money either: $3,470,000 is committed against $130,000 of room in the entire league, so the owners with the biggest holes are largely the owners who cannot do anything about them. Whitman has nothing at all. Creelman has barely more.

The forecast holds 7 starters out of week one, and two more by hand: Sam Darnold for week two and A.J. Brown for weeks two through four, both hurt in the opener and neither of them listed yet. The one that lands on a contender is Brock Bowers, C-Town's tight end, whose knee needed a trimmed meniscus. Eric Martel thought the report made surgery sound like a haircut.

As for week one itself: four NFL teams had finished when this was put together. The owners whose players happen to have played already look better than the owners whose players have not, and that is the entire content of it. Klingenberg is currently crediting Blake with breaking injury news faster than Schefter. That is the level of information available in September.

The Divisions, Strongest to Weakest

The owners drew these divisions themselves and they are stuck with them through 2028, which is what makes the argument about which came out strongest worth having. On the simulation's arithmetic it is Outlast PG Time: 52.5% across Pure Greatness, HammerTime and The Long Haulers, and it spent less at the auction than any other division. Cross pitched a name for it in June, two recent champions and a problem, casting himself as the problem. The name that stuck was Outlast PG Time. The forecast kept his joke anyway: the champions are second and fourth in the league, and Cross is 9th.

DOG POUND 4 LIFE is a hair behind at 51.6% and holds more title equity than anyone at 30.8%. Two thirds of that is Dunn by himself, with Mannila and Klingenberg behind him. It is the division with the best team in it and not the best three.

Maddie Mondays and LEGION OF BOOM finish level, at 48.1% and 47.8%, and they are level in completely different ways. Maddie Mondays is the tightest division in the league and the one that spent the most at the auction: Creelman, Josh Martel and Bousquet, nobody clear of anybody. LEGION OF BOOM is the widest: Blake third in the league, Gerardi twelfth, Whitman in between. That is a floor problem rather than a ceiling one.

None of this is a promise. Backtested against the last five seasons the model explained roughly two fifths of the spread in final all-play and ranked the field only loosely in each of the last three, so read it as odds. Gride finishes top three in a real share of the 20,000 runs and C-Town finishes last in a few of them. Year one of three starts now.
