L.A. Looks Fielded the Best Defense in the League and Sold the Offense to Pay for It

Ryan Bousquet ranks 1 of 12 in points per started defender and 10 of 12 overall. In between those two numbers is a July trade that sent his best receiver out the door one day before the roster locked.

The Lineup

Start with the good news, because it is genuinely good and almost nobody in this league gets credit for it. Danielle Hunter grades Mid DL1 and Jeffery Simmons High-end DL2. Zack Baun is a Mid LB1 with Jamien Sherwood at High-end LB2 beside him. Kamren Curl is a High-end DB1 and Trevon Moehrig a High-end DB2. That is six defensive starters and not one of them is a passenger.

It is not an accident either. Over the life of the league Bousquet has acquired 257 defenders, ranking 2, and more to the point he has converted them: 1 of 12 in points per started defender, which is first, outright, ahead of every owner who works the wire harder than he does. Working it hardest and scoring most are different skills and he has the second one.

Now the offense, where the wheels are visibly off. Bo Nix grades High-end QB2 and Sam Darnold Low-end QB2, which is a functional superflex pair and ranks 9. Chase Brown is a Low-end RB1 and then it stops: Jaylen Warren is a Replacement-level RB and the position ranks 10. George Pickens is a Low-end WR1, Alec Pierce and Courtland Sutton are both Low-end WR3, and the room still comes out 10. Jake Ferguson is a Replacement-level TE, Isaiah Likely is another one in the flex, and tight end ranks 7 mostly because everyone else is also miserable there.

The last flex is filled by Aaron Donald, who the production model cannot value at all because he has no prior season to measure. That is not a starter. That is a slot the optimizer refused to leave blank.

Add it up and the composite lands 10 of 12: 2 of the priced starting slots hold a top-twelve player and 3 hold nobody the board ranks at all. This is not a team that is merely thin. It is a very good defense with an offense bolted to it.

The Bridge

In April this roster had 15 active players and could not legally fill a lineup, so there was no lineup to speak of and no point pretending otherwise. Contracts expire here and rosters empty out; that is the cycle working, not a crisis. What matters is what he did with the room, and he had $84,000 committed to start with.

By the time the roster locked ahead of the auction he was at 21 active and $137,000 committed, and the priced slots had barely moved. Bo Nix at quarterback, Chase Brown at the top of the backfield, Alec Pierce and Courtland Sutton at receiver, Jake Ferguson at tight end. The same names, in the same order, doing the same amount of not-very-much. Legal-lineup value crept from 13,000 to 16,044.

The auction is where the roster actually got built: 35 active when it closed, 21,938 of lineup value, holes down from 6 to 3. George Pickens replaced Alec Pierce at the top of the receiver room and Pierce slid down a slot, which is the single biggest change on the sheet. Jaylen Warren took the second back spot from Zach Charbonnet.

Studs went from 1 to 2 and have stayed at 2 since. One extra top-twelve player for a full offseason of work is a thin return, and it is the honest summary of the whole exercise: he did not get worse, he got wider. There is now a body in every slot. Most of them are the wrong bodies.

The Auctions

Two separate auctions, months apart, and he treated them very differently.

At the Expired Rookie Auction in May he won 4 lots for $16,000 out of a pool of 14 players league-wide. Zach Charbonnet went to him at $7,000, which was the most expensive player anyone bought in that auction. That is worth keeping in proportion: the ERA sells men whose rookie deals ran out without earning a second one, and its top price would not have cracked the middle of the free-agent board. Tank Bigsby, Jordan Battle and Marvin Mims came with him.

The Free Agent Auction is the real market and he spent like it, $123,000 across 14 lots. $48,000 of that went on George Pickens alone, 39.0% of everything he spent there, against a league-high price of $88,000 paid elsewhere.

Hold that Pickens number for the trade section, because it does not read the same once you know what he sold to afford it. The rest of the sheet is sensible enough: Daniel Jones as quarterback insurance, Jaylen Warren for the backfield, Isaiah Likely at tight end, and then a run of defenders — Jeffery Simmons, Danielle Hunter, Zack Baun, Jamien Sherwood, Trevon Moehrig — that between them give him a defense that grades well almost everywhere, for $22,000, or 15.8% of his auction money. He ranks 8 of 12 on that share, which is to say he did it without overpaying for it.

Contracts, Trades, Cuts and the Rookie Class

7 contract moves, 4 trades, 4 roster removals and $0 of cap penalty. The paperwork is clean. The judgement is where it goes wrong.

Chase Brown got a genuine extension and Alec Pierce got another. Charbonnet, Pickens, Mims and Moehrig were converted to multi-year deals off their auction wins, and only Mims is at the league minimum — the others are not, whatever the small numbers next to them suggest. Sam Darnold was restructured, though the extension underneath that restructure was Gride's work, done before Bousquet ever owned him, and does not belong on his ledger.

Which brings us to the deal. One day before the roster locked for the auction he sent Amon-Ra St. Brown, Shedeur Sanders and $10,000 of blind-bid money to Gride, and got back Sam Darnold, Jalen Coker and Emmett Johnson. St. Brown was the largest contract he traded away all offseason, at $51,000, and he is a Gride player now, on a tag, at exactly that price.

So he cleared $51,000 and then walked into the auction and spent $48,000 on George Pickens, who grades Low-end WR1. He round-tripped almost the identical money into a demonstrably worse receiver, and paid Shedeur Sanders and $10,000 in cash for the privilege. If the plan was cap relief, the relief lasted one auction. If the plan was Darnold, we will come back to Darnold.

The other three trades are better. David Montgomery went to HammerTime in April for a 2027 second-rounder, which is a fine price for a back he was not going to start. A pick swap with the same partner turned 3.08 into 4.08, 5.01 and a 2027 fourth, and both of those picks became players he still holds. The July deal with The Long Haulers is the one that stings quietly: Jordan Battle was a top-twelve defensive back and he went out for Isaiah Bond, who the redraft board does not price at all.

The removals barely register. One contract cut, a waiver swap, and 2 bodies signed and discarded inside the same offseason. Nothing was charged against the cap.

The rookie class is a lottery ticket collection: 6 picks, none earlier than 3.10, where the historical hit rate is already poor and every pick after it is worse. Bryce Lance at 3.10 is the best of them by baseline. David Bailey at 4.08 and Emmanuel McNeil-Warren at 6.01 are defenders, which means neither the redraft board nor the production model has any opinion on them yet — not a bad grade, no grade at all.

The Verdict

He plays in a division of 3 and he finishes 3 in it. Sex Manther and Real Deal Creel are both ahead of him league-wide, and he plays each of them twice. That is the part that actually decides a seed, and it is not close.

Is this the strongest team in the league? No — 10 of 12 says it plainly. Was it a good offseason? Also no, and for a specific reason rather than a general one. He spent $139,000 across both auctions and sits on $5,000 of room against $295,000 committed, and the single most consequential thing he did was hand his best offensive player to Gride and buy a lesser version of him back at nearly the same price.

The biggest advantage is the defense, and it is not a small one: Hunter, Simmons, Baun, Sherwood, Curl and Moehrig, first in the league at converting defenders into points. The biggest weakness is every skill position on the other side of the ball, where 3 of nine priced slots hold a player the board does not rank, and the receiver room is led by a Low-end WR1 who cost $48,000.

One more thing, and it is only one game. 2 NFL teams had played when this was written, and Sam Darnold — the quarterback the St. Brown trade was built around, carried at the price of a starter — banked 0.2 points, on a passing line you can read in full in the table below, before Drew Lock finished the game for Seattle. Nobody has designated him anything and one afternoon proves nothing. It is simply the first live evidence on the bet, and it did not open well.
